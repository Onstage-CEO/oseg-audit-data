The defining entertainment finding at The Phoenician Resort and Spa is a placement problem, not a programming one. A guest who booked the Phoenician suite rated the stay two stars because the room sits directly below the on-site steakhouse, where late music and noise carried into the room well past a reasonable hour. That single detail captures the situation precisely: the resort's live entertainment is good enough that guests notice it and praise it, but its physical relationship to guest rooms is not fully managed.

Entertainment is a focused part of the conversation here, not the headline. It appears in roughly seven percent of reviews, well behind the themes that define this property for its guests, service and staff, food and beverage, and the pool and amenities. Within that smaller conversation, in-venue entertainment rates about on par with the resort's overall standard, so the work is not a turnaround. It is targeting the specific venues and moments that pull above or below the line.

The pattern across the reviews is clear. The bars and lounges and the restaurants are where entertainment lives at The Phoenician, and the live music in those spaces is doing real work for the guest experience. The watch-item is acoustic, where that music reaches rooms it should not.

The satisfaction landscape

The theme mix at The Phoenician is led decisively by service and staff, which appears in close to half of all reviews. Food and beverage and the pool and amenities follow, both well ahead of everything else. Cleanliness and room, value, and check-in wait times sit in the middle of the conversation. Entertainment and atmosphere is the smallest of the named themes.

For a luxury resort in Scottsdale, this is a healthy distribution. Guests come for service and dining and the grounds, and they talk about those things most. Entertainment is not what brings them through the door, which is exactly why it functions as a focused, high-leverage lever rather than a broad problem. The live music that does appear is woven into the venues guests already rate highly, the lounges and the restaurants, so improvements there compound an experience guests are already inclined to praise.

Entertainment deep-dive

In-venue entertainment at The Phoenician rates about on par with the property's overall standard, so the value is in the specific venues rather than a single verdict. Three settings carry the entertainment conversation: the bars and lounges, which draw the most mentions, the restaurants close behind, and the pool decks and common areas.

The live music in the bars and lounges is the clearest strength. Guests single out the named lounge for live music alongside reasonable pricing, and a lively happy hour with music in the lobby reads as a genuine atmosphere win. In the restaurants, the picture is more mixed in a useful way. Afternoon high tea is praised for its food and service, with piano music as part of the ambiance, which shows the resort already uses live music to lift a daytime dining experience. The same restaurant strength, however, is the source of the watch-item: the steakhouse music that delights diners is the music that carried into the suite directly below it.

That is the pattern worth acting on. The entertainment is well received where guests choose to be near it, the lounge, the lobby happy hour, the tea service, and a problem only where it reaches guests who did not choose it, in the rooms above the steakhouse. The fix is about sound containment and scheduling discipline in the dining venues, not about changing what is performed.

The guest voice

The honest read is that most guests who mention entertainment praise it, and a smaller, real group did not. Of the guests who spoke to the in-venue experience, fifty rated it five stars while eight rated it one or two. The praise centers on live music in the lounge and lobby and on the ambiance of the tea service. The critical voice is narrower and more specific: it is largely the guest in the suite below the steakhouse, where music and late noise undercut an expensive room. Those eight low-star mentions are the practical case for the sound work below, not a sign that the program is failing.

The opportunity

The opportunity at The Phoenician is to protect and extend a program guests already like, while closing the one acoustic gap that produced a two-star stay. Three grounded moves follow from the reviews.

First, treat the steakhouse-to-room sound issue as a defined fix. The complaint is specific and repeatable for any guest booking the suite below it, so resolving the timing and containment of live music in that venue removes a recurring risk to the highest-value rooms.

Second, build on what works in the bars and lounges. The named lounge and the lobby happy hour already earn praise for live music. Consistent, well-staged programming in those spaces is the lowest-risk place to raise the entertainment ceiling, because guests are already responding to it.

Third, extend the daytime model that the high tea proves out. Live piano lifting a dining experience is something guests notice and value, and it points to room for similar light, room-appropriate music in other daytime and pool-deck moments. Across all three, the role for one partner managing the booking, the staging, and the sound is to keep entertainment an asset in the venues guests choose, and never a disturbance in the rooms above them.
