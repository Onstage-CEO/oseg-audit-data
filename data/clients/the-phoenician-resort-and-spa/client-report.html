1. Executive diagnosis

The Phoenician has entertainment assets, but not yet an entertainment system.

The property owns entertainment that guests genuinely love. Dueling pianos at The Phoenician Tavern, live music at Thirsty Camel, and a resident pianist at Afternoon Tea each draw specific, five-star affection from the guests who encounter them. These are not incidental. They are real assets, and when a guest meets one of them, it can define the evening.

The problem is that these wins are not connected to each other or delivered on purpose. They reach only the guests who happen to be in the right venue on the right night. Programs that appear on the property's own schedule do not always show up in the guest voice, and at least one entertainment layer that guests once praised has gone quiet. The result is a resort where entertainment rates a step below the property's own high standard, not because the talent is weak, but because there is no system making it consistent, visible, and deliberate across the guest experience.

That gap is narrow, specific, and fixable. This report shows where entertainment is working, where the system breaks down, and why the next step is an on-site diagnostic rather than another round of review reading.

2. What the evidence shows

Guests judge The Phoenician on service and the pool. Those two themes dominate what they choose to write about, well ahead of food and beverage, the room, and value. Entertainment and atmosphere sit at the back of that list, raised in roughly one review in ten.

Two things follow from that ordering. First, entertainment is not what defines a stay here, which is precisely why it is a focused, high-leverage lever rather than the headline. Second, and more important, the guests who do raise entertainment split more sharply than the property's overall four-star standing would predict. An equal number rate their entertainment experience at the top and at the bottom of the scale. For a resort that holds a solid four-star reputation on the strength of its service and grounds, that divergence is the signal worth reading closely: the ceiling is high, and the floor is lower than the rest of the property.

Read plainly, entertainment is the one theme trailing the standard The Phoenician sets everywhere else. It is not a crisis. It is a quiet drag on scores the property should be lifting.

3. Where entertainment is working

The property already proves it can deliver entertainment guests remember and write about in detail. All three assets below are verified active in Onstage's booking records, the strongest source available for this property, since Onstage books its entertainment: each program is on the books today with confirmed future dates. The guest voice is documented alongside each as the response layer. Each claim is tied to a specific venue, program, evidence window, and source, following the source hierarchy set out in the appendix, in which booking-system data outranks public review recency for the venues Onstage books.

These assets share a pattern. Each is a specific, named touchpoint. Each lands strongly with the guests who encounter it. And each is confined largely to a single venue and the guests who happen to be there. That is the good news and the constraint in the same breath: the quality is there, and its reach is not.

4. Where the system breaks down

The distance between those wins and how entertainment reads across the property is where the work is. Four fault lines account for most of it.

Reach and consistency

The signature acts only reach guests who are in the right venue on the right evening. There is no visible thread carrying that energy across the property, and the wider body of entertainment mentions divides far more evenly between praise and criticism than the resort's overall reputation should allow. One guest captures the tension in a single line, pairing a compliment about the music with a complaint about cost, which places entertainment inside a value conversation instead of letting it stand on its own.

“Extremely expensive food and drinks in bar. Music was great”

Google, 2 stars, 2026-01-09

A program that ran for years without registering in the guest voice: the Mowry & Cotton brunch DJ

Status: historical, previously active; retired December 2025 (Onstage booking records, source level 1). The Sunday brunch DJ at Mowry & Cotton ran as a booked Onstage program through December 28, 2025, when it was discontinued; the venue's 2026 entertainment is holiday and event one-offs. The striking finding is what the guest voice did with those years of programming: in the venue's 1,162 OpenTable reviews, the brunch DJ is named in dated reviews only across late 2018 to January 2020, and the most recent mention is January 19, 2020. The program kept running for nearly six more years without registering in a single dated guest review. A 2023 review that contains the letters “DJ” refers to a server by name, not a disc jockey, and is excluded.

The honest read is sharper than a staffing question: this was live entertainment spend that guests stopped noticing six years before it stopped running. That is the visibility failure this report keeps finding in different forms, a real program with no system making it seen, and it is the pattern to fix before any program is reinstated. Whether a redesigned, visible brunch program earns its way back is a decision the on-site diagnostic can put numbers behind.

“We loved everything about this restaurant except for one thing: the DJ had her music volume so high we couldn't hear each other from one end of the table of six to another.”

OpenTable, 5 stars, 2020-01-19 (most recent dated DJ mention on record)

A proven asset that has gone quiet: J&G Steakhouse live music

Status: historical, previously active; now holiday-only. J&G Steakhouse is the clearest example of entertainment the property has already validated and is not currently using on a regular basis. Onstage previously booked regular live music here and now books it on a holiday basis only, which is operator-confirmed context at the top of the source hierarchy, and the dated guest voice matches that arc. Across its 3,436 OpenTable reviews, live music, a band, a guitarist, or a singer is mentioned in reviews dated from 2013 through 2025, and roughly four in five of those reviews are four or five stars. Guests describe live music on Friday and Saturday evenings as soothing, romantic, and the thing that made the evening complete.

“Great live music on Fridays and Saturdays. Tuna tartar a must. The steaks great but the Béarnaise sauce was wow. Wine list good. Overall amazing dinner a great place a night out with your loved one.”

Yelp, 5 stars

Date-filtering the mentions shows a clear arc. They cluster through the venue's active years and peak in 2022 (twelve dated live-music mentions), thin to five in 2023 and four in 2024, and by 2025 the only live-music mention on record is a Thanksgiving brunch. The most recent dated mention of a regular evening act is August 16, 2024. In other words, a layer guests once loved has quieted from a weekly act into occasional or holiday-only programming. The honest read is not that J&G has no entertainment relevance. It is that a genuinely liked layer is now dormant on a regular basis, which makes it a candidate for selective, evidence-led reactivation on specific evenings or holidays, guided by the historical dates above rather than assumption. As with the Tavern, the evidence carries an honest tradeoff: a real minority found the bar-area music too loud for dinner, which is a calibration point, not a reason to stay away.

Blind spots the review data cannot see

Some of the property's highest-potential entertainment surfaces barely register in written reviews at all, which is a limit of the medium rather than evidence of absence. Poolside energy, family and kids programming such as the Funicians Club and dive-in movies, and seasonal and holiday activations are exactly the kind of experiences guests live rather than review. Whether they are intentionally designed, consistently staffed, visible to the right guests, and aligned with expectations is not something review data can answer. It is something an on-site diagnostic is built to answer.

5. Strategic opportunity

The opportunity is not to add entertainment. The property already has assets guests love. The opportunity is to turn those assets into a system: to make what works consistent and reachable, to reactivate what has been proven and left dormant, and to deliberately design the guest-experience surfaces that review data cannot see.

The business case is direct. Entertainment is the single theme trailing the standard The Phoenician sets on service and the pool, which means it is quietly capping guest scores the property is otherwise earning. A resort whose guests already rate it on atmosphere and experience has more to gain from sharpening that atmosphere than almost any other lever available to it. Closing this gap does not require a large new program. It requires making the existing strengths deliberate, extending their reach, and confirming what is actually happening on the ground.

The specific, validated, and costed plan for doing that is the subject of the on-site diagnostic. This audit establishes that the opportunity is real and worth the property's attention. It does not attempt to prescribe the full roadmap from review data alone, because the most valuable moves depend on what the next step confirms in person.

6. Why an on-site diagnostic is required

Guest reviews are a powerful lens, and they have a hard boundary. They tell you where guests noticed something and how they felt about it. They cannot tell you what is running right now versus what is merely listed, whether a program is designed or incidental, whether a paid touchpoint is landing or quietly missing, or how the experiences guests live rather than write about actually perform.

This property sits precisely on that boundary. Onstage's booking records already settle what is running in the venues Onstage books: a brunch DJ that ran for years without registering in the guest voice before it was retired, and a beloved live-music layer at J&G now reduced to holidays. What the data cannot resolve is how the property-run layer, pool, family, tours, and each seasonal activation, actually lands for guests. Answering that requires being on-site, seeing what runs, and reading how guests respond in the moment.

7. What Onstage would evaluate on-site

The on-site diagnostic would validate what is actually running, where guests notice it, and where the highest-value opportunities are being missed. In particular, it would evaluate:

  • Active versus listed programming. The Onstage-booked layer is already established by booking records, including what has been retired, such as the Mowry & Cotton brunch DJ, discontinued December 2025. Confirm the currency and on-the-ground quality of the property-run layer: the garden and art tours, dive-in movies, and related listed programs.
  • Signature-act reach. Assess how the proven wins at The Phoenician Tavern and Thirsty Camel can extend to more guests, more evenings, and more of the property, rather than reaching only those in the right place at the right time.
  • Pool and poolside programming. Evaluate whether poolside energy is intentionally designed, visible, and matched to guest expectations in peak summer, when the pool is the center of the guest experience. Live poolside music is currently a holiday special (Memorial Day, July 4th, Labor Day weekends), not a standing program; whether that cadence matches peak-summer guest expectations is precisely the question to answer on-site.
  • Family and kids programming. Assess the Funicians Club, dive-in movies, and related family activations as a likely high-value experience layer that written reviews cannot fully diagnose.
  • Seasonal and holiday programming. Examine whether holiday and seasonal activations, from summer through the winter holidays, are consistent, entertainment-forward, and aligned with the property's standard.
  • Dormant-asset reactivation. Test whether the previously loved live-music layer at J&G Steakhouse warrants selective reactivation on specific evenings or holidays, guided by the historical guest evidence rather than assumption.
  • Experience calibration. Resolve the bar-area volume tradeoff at The Phoenician Tavern, where 18% of dueling-piano mentions sit on reviews of 3 stars or below, so live music elevates dining rather than competing with it. The same pattern appears historically in J&G's archived reviews.

The validated findings and the costed, prioritized plan that follows from them are delivered as the output of the on-site diagnostic.

Methodology

This report draws on 282 verified public guest reviews of The Phoenician Resort and Spa across Booking.com, TripAdvisor, Expedia, and Google, scored over a trailing twelve-month window, and supported by outlet-level venue signal for The Phoenician Tavern, Thirsty Camel, Afternoon Tea, Mowry & Cotton, and J&G Steakhouse. Findings reflect what guests wrote in their own words on public platforms. Theme shares, ratings, and mention counts are rendered directly from the review sets rather than estimated. Where the report notes that a program is dormant, listed but unconfirmed, or an on-site diagnostic area, that reflects the boundary of what review data can establish; those questions are resolved by the on-site diagnostic, not by inference from reviews. Current-programming status labels follow the source hierarchy set out above the programming table: for venues Onstage books, Onstage's operator-confirmed booking data is the primary source and outranks public review recency, and public review evidence is treated as a guest-response layer rather than proof that a program is or is not running.
